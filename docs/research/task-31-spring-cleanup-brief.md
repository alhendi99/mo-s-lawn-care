# Task 31 research brief — Des Moines spring cleanup checklist

Research date: 2026-09-02 (Asia/Amman). Governing editorial policy: `docs/blog-writing-guidance.md`. Scope: Task 31 only; baseline `5327b2f`. No deployment or external-account changes.

## User intent

Decide what deserves attention on a Des Moines-area property as winter gives way to active spring yard care, what should wait for better conditions, and which lawn decisions are separate from cleanup. This is a property-review checklist, not Mo's Spring Cleanup package, a treatment plan, or a year-round calendar.

## Research scope

Investigated Iowa guidance for winter debris, perennial-bed cleanup, wet soil, emerging plants, brown or matted turf, mowing restart, aeration, seeding, and the limits of a generic spring checklist. Also audited current City of Des Moines/Metro Waste Authority disposal resources, current same-intent terminology, and a small representative result set.

Chemical programs, fertilizer prescriptions, mower maintenance, pruning instructions, irrigation, gutters, equipment, fixed dates, frost/soil-temperature thresholds, current weather, broad municipal operations, and every unverified Mo's service inclusion are excluded from published advice. Turf guidance, garden-bed guidance, municipal guidance, and business capability remain separate evidence systems.

### Freshness and conflict review

- S1 was reviewed in March 2026 and directly covers both the Iowa lawn debris question and the narrower perennial-bed conditions. It supersedes the need to rely on the old 2001 Iowa State spring-cleaning article.
- S2 remains linked from Iowa State's lawn resource directory, last reviewed February 2025. Its detailed fertilizer, herbicide, irrigation and equipment content is outside Task 31 and not imported.
- S1 and S2 both reject a universal first-mow date by tying mowing to growth/type. Task 30 already owns the detailed frequency/removal explanation, so Task 31 retains only the transition.
- S1 says spring cleanup can include lawn debris removal after snow has melted. It does not authorize aggressive whole-lawn raking, power raking or dethatching. S3 supports gentle raking only for the described affected patches.
- S1 warns against work in wet perennial-bed soil. A current University of Minnesota Extension result also warned against lawn work on soggy soil, but its northern-Minnesota scope was not needed and was not imported as Iowa authority. The public wet-soil statement remains explicitly about beds; aeration moisture conditions come from S4.
- S1 notes beneficial/native insects may overwinter in perennial debris and openly says the population effect of waiting is not yet well known. The public article preserves that uncertainty and does not create a rigid temperature threshold.
- S2 says spring seeding is possible but prefers September; S5 explains the late-summer advantage and spring weed competition. Task 29 owns the full overseeding decision, so Task 31 only says seeding is not an automatic cleanup item.
- S1/S2 allow April aeration; S4 says aeration depends on lawn use/soil and soil should be moist, not dry or wet. Task 28 owns the timing and need decision, so Task 31 does not prescribe aeration.
- S6 is live and directs current City of Des Moines yard-waste questions to Des Moines Public Works. The lack of a visible update date is handled by linking to the current resource rather than publishing operational details.
- The February 2, 2026 Metro Waste Authority program notice and the City/2026 SCRUB resources were re-opened/audited. Their dates, fees, bags, collection windows, event eligibility and drop-off instructions are too volatile for the evergreen checklist and are not published.

## Differentiation brief

### Commodity expectations

Readers reasonably expect help reviewing leftover debris, turf condition, beds and the transition to mowing. Those subjects remain, but only where source-supported.

### Actual information gap

Current results rarely explain which task depends on the observed surface, which task should wait, which “spring chore” is actually a separate lawn-care decision, and which disposal answer depends on exact jurisdiction.

### Task 31 unique value

1. A four-state property review: **clear**, **observe**, **wait**, or **decide separately**. It encodes action and restraint instead of a chore count.
2. Separate lawn and perennial-bed conditions so a wet-bed warning is not falsely generalized to every property surface.
3. Treat matted/brown patches as an observation requiring cautious follow-up, not proof that the entire lawn needs aggressive raking or renovation.
4. Route mowing, aeration and seeding to their own decisions; cleanup does not automatically authorize them.
5. Keep the City-only disposal verification path separate from Iowa horticultural guidance and from Mo's business scope.

Planned structure: direct answer; one property-review checklist; observation section for turf patches; “three separate decisions” section; municipality verification; one restrained service-scope transition. No month-by-month calendar, factor table, pass/result loop, three-question readiness formula, FAQ, generic benefits/mistakes, or recap.
